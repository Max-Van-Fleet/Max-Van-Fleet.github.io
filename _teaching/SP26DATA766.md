# About The Course and My Role

DATA 766 and DATA 481 are paired courses in which graduate students and advanced undergraduates enrolled in DATA 766 serve as team leads for semester-long research projects proposed by external clients, while students enrolled in DATA 481 work as members of those research teams.

Most class meetings were organized around individual team sessions, with periodic full-cohort meetings where all teams shared their progress and received feedback. As a team lead, I was responsible for communicating with our client, coordinating the team’s work, assigning tasks, supporting students as they encountered technical challenges, and ensuring that the project remained on schedule.

I organized our meetings using a structure loosely adapted from the Scrum framework. Each meeting began with a brief stand-up in which team members reported their progress, described their next steps, and identified any bottlenecks. A rotating student scribe recorded meeting minutes and action items. I then assigned work to be completed before the next meeting, provided the resources or instruction needed to support those tasks, and worked individually with team members to resolve the challenges raised during the stand-up.

# About The Project

Our team analyzed a phenotypic screening dataset for *Caenorhabditis elegans* (*C. elegans*) provided by Linnea Wethekam, a postdoctoral researcher in the Department of Biology at UNC–Chapel Hill. The project’s goal was to identify genes with similar functional roles by comparing the phenotypic effects produced when each gene was knocked down.

We explored several dimensionality-reduction methods, including t-SNE, UMAP, and PHATE, alongside a broad range of clustering techniques. These included Leiden and Louvain community detection, Gaussian and Bernoulli mixture models, k-means clustering, consensus clustering, and hierarchical clustering.

Our analysis indicated that the available data were insufficient to support strong statistically significant conclusions about many of the proposed gene relationships. We also found that fully automated clustering methods often produced groups that were too broad to be biologically informative. The results suggested that the most useful workflow would combine computational methods with expert biological review, allowing a domain specialist to refine candidate gene networks using dendrograms and other low-dimensional representations.
